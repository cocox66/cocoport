Becoming

a short story · Coco Xia, June 2026

He perches with pride on the canopy of an oak tree, gazing out at the campus he calls home. He sees students – giants – in the distance giggling and chattering on a picnic blanket on the lawn, and massive, two-wheeled machines rolling down the streets below. He’s waiting for his lover to return to the nest, a little home they’ve built together with the fresh moss, dandelion fluff, and spider silk they collect every morning when the grass is still damp from the dewdrops. His iridescent feathers glisten in the early spring sunlight.

He was born in a small but sturdy nest in a bush in front of a building named SAPP Center for Science Teaching and Learning, where his early mornings were entertained yet also startled at times by the sounds of rolling wheels, loud chattering, and sickly coughs by another species which he later learned to be humans. He would feast on the nectar and tiny June bugs brought over by his loving mother every day for lunch and dinner, and when he finally fledged, he was the most beautiful hummingbird in the neighborhood. With a beautiful emerald-green plumage, a shimmery neck, and one of the fastest wingbeat rates among his peers, he was quite a catch.

On the day he turned one year old, he met the girl of his dreams. She hovered before him like a jewel sprung loose from the throat of spring. Her feathers flashed a sophisticated green and a tender rose gold in the light, and she carried in her beak a single absurdly oversized strand of dry grass, as though personally determined to build the most beautiful home ever seen to birdkind. He offered to help her with it but she refused. He admired this immediately.

He decided to pursue her right away by finding ever larger chunks of dry grass, combined with the world-class spider silk he gathered from his friend, a renowned yellow garden spider named Gandolf. Together, they built a nest tucked into the crook of a branch overlooking the wide human pathways. He liked to think it was one of the finest homes on campus: excellent ventilation, strong morning light, and only occasional disturbances from skateboarders. Some evenings they would sit together and watch the giants below return to their stone dens, dragging black sacks twenty times the size of either of them. They prayed to Goddess Demetera, the feathered goddess of earth and health, every night before they slept, wishing their children a healthy childhood and carefree upbringing.

He never quite understood why the giants spent their days going to something called “school.” He knew the stone buildings were where something called knowledge gets spread and minds enlightened, he’d heard the giants say so themselves, but school was never something he’d had to attend. He has always been a strong believer of learning through living. He’d learned how a kestrel chases hummingbirds, seen how a praying mantis can snatch his best friend right out of the air, a creature so small and seemingly harmless that he should’ve known its bright colors were a warning. That lesson had cost him a friend, but he knows it forever now, written into his body, not onto some page he’d have to carry around in a sack. And so the giants baffled him. They hauled those sacks into fluorescent rooms to memorize the thoughts of other giants, many of whom, he gathered, were already dead: long-gone philosophers and scholars whose ideas the living ones recited back like prayers and hymns. Imagine, he thought, learning how to live from someone who was no longer alive. That’s so funny. What’s the point? They stored away mountains of knowledge they would surely never use, while a kestrel could teach you everything that mattered in a single afternoon, if you could survive that lesson.

Then one afternoon, perched on a small tree, he noticed a girl with a phone pressed to her ear. She wore a floral sweater the color of a tulip’s insides, raspberry pink blending into a warm, buttery yellow, the kind the giants wore in spring. She was crying to her mother, delicate pearls rolling down her cheeks, grieving over something called a midterm she had not done well on. Her voice was stripped of the confidence he has usually heard from giants her age. This baffled him even more than the sacks of dead men’s thoughts he’d observed earlier. He had seen this girl work on a bench before, and he knew how hard she had scribbled on her notebook and whispered to herself to explain every problem. In his world, you measured yourself against your own yesterday, had you beaten your wing faster than the day before, were you trying your best today. By that measure she had already succeeded; her effort was obvious and undeniable. Yet there she was, grieving over a dead number, as though the trying counted for nothing. He could not understand it. He wanted, absurdly, to tell her that she should be proud.

As the girl took off on her two-wheeled machine, he followed her from a distance, hovering after the mellow colors on her sweater as it moved across the campus. She stopped at a patch of lawn where some other giants were already congregating, spread across the grass in a careless manner, like little marble stones scattered on the board. She threw her machine to the side, and waved at them. When they saw her, they all let out a sound – a genuine, caring exclamation. Someone gave her a blanket and tucked her a little, someone pressed a snack into her hands. Someone muttered some words that most certainly was a joke as she burst into giggles, her wet eyes from minutes ago already drying though she seemed unsure of what exactly was funny. A boy a few giants to her right had headphones around his neck and fed chips to himself. Another lay asleep, mouth open, one arm flung over her eyes, more comfortable than ever. He watched them the same way he sometimes watched the clouds, with no expectation and pure appreciation, except now he had gotten interested in something else: how the girl mourning a number a moment ago, somehow, seemed to be fine in the blink of an eye.

So far, he had discovered nothing that humans had in common with what he recognized from his own kind. There was no courtship display here, no competition. The way male hummingbirds dove and chittered and flashed their gorgets to be chosen. These creatures simply were together, orbiting each other loosely, like small warm planets. He understood territory, mating, but never this: the way humans chose, again and again, to simply be near each other and in a group, just for the extraordinary comfort of being seen and understood. A hummingbird would have starved waiting around like that. His kind had to feed almost every waking minute, with no hours to spare lying in the grass. He could not decide whether that made these giants foolish or free.

As the sun kept quietly moving, the girl headed towards a small fountain, its water trickling clear and soft. A boy with paint-stained fingers sat there waiting. When she saw him, something lifted in her step, and she walked as if she was surrounded by happy fairies.

He took a snapshot of the scene that followed: her head tipped against his shoulder, his hand loosely holding hers, both of them saying nothing, just existing as the breeze passed gently over their faces. Because his heart beat a thousand times a minute, and because he had lived on this campus too long, he could see the whole arc of them at once. He could see the months that would follow, the late nights, the inside jokes that would become their private dialect, the way she would save him a seat in the class where they learned their own body’s anatomy and grew bioluminescent peonies together. He perched there, thinking he would never want to study hummingbird anatomy, let alone learn it from the skeleton of a dead one. That would frighten him. Yet it broke his heart a little to also see the specific Thursday in June, when the silence between them would start to feel different, when they would begin treating each other politely and carefully, having agreed to something quietly devastating.

He could see all of it, but he did not pity them.

He had spent that day confused about her, how she’d wept over a number, by the giants who hauled their heavy sacks into fluorescent rooms to acquire knowledge. But watching this boy tuck a strand of hair behind her ear with the concentrated tenderness of someone trying to memorize something, he understood that this too was something you could not learn from a book. You had to be in it, in the moment. You had to let the color in, even knowing the canvas wouldn’t last. Something started to click for him.

In his world, love was efficient. You build a nest, raise a young. The seasons moved. He loved his mate the same way he loved the silk woven into their home, for its strength, its function, its beauty and resilience under pressure. But what he was watching now was different. It was love as practice, love as the first rough draft of something these giants were still learning to write. And perhaps that was not a lesser thing. Perhaps that was its own particular miracle: the willingness to give your whole fast-beating heart away to something you might already sense is temporary. To build the nest anyway, because what if it will last through the thunderstorms? He was starting to understand why.

Demetera, he whispered. What a brave and reckless species.

As the sun dipped, he couldn’t help but find her again, the floral sweater, a little wrinkled now from the long day, standing at the far side of the building, where the path curved and most giants didn’t bother to walk. She stood in front of nothing. There was no audience, no companion, just a wall and her speaking to herself.

He landed on a low branch nearby and tilted his head.

She was saying what sounded like the same sequence of words over and over, in a voice that started uncertain and then cracked open into something enormous. She would stop, shake her head, and begin again. Her hands moved differently each time, searching for the exact right angle of gesture. This puzzled him more than anything else she had done all day. A hummingbird wanted to be watched, the whole point of a gorget was to flash it, to dive and chitter and be chosen. Yet here she was, hiding where no one could see her, terrified, it seemed, of the very thing he lived for: being looked at. He watched her fail and restart perhaps eleven times, her voice shrinking each time it faltered, as though some old doubt were whispering. It was the same voice that had cracked on the phone that morning over the bad grade. Yet only this time, she didn’t give up and kept going. The twelfth time, she took a slight pause in the middle of it, and then something happened to her face, not a smile exactly, but a kind of flow state, and she finished the rest without stopping.

She stood there for a moment in the afternoon light, stunned by her own performance.

She exhaled. She opened her notes and wrote something down. Then she laughed, once, short, to herself, did some jumping jacks, tucked the papers under her arm, and walked back toward the stone buildings.

This he had seen in his own world before. The young osprey the first time it dove down at a rodent and actually caught it. The moment robins flapped their wings for the first time and fledged out of their nests. The very moment the body could simply do the thing it was supposed to do. It was always private, not necessarily a loud moment. The giant had a word for it that he overheard from an open window: becoming.

He stayed on the branch for a while after she left. The air where she had been still felt charged, the way air does after a bolt of lightning. He thought about his own becoming, the first morning he had hovered long enough without wobbling, the day his feathers finally felt full and his wings found their rhythm. And when the whole sky finally opened up, so did every challenge, every kestrel and kite. And this girl, who he had spent a whole day observing, chose to go forward anyway. Watching her walk away in her wrinkled sweater brought forward an enlightenment to him: the quiet determination in her eyes, the steady confidence in his steps. He suddenly understood what resolution really meant.

The sun was angling lower now, slowly slipping behind the rooftops, and he heard her before he saw her, the quick percussion of her wings, familiar as his own heartbeat. His face immediately lit up.

She landed beside him on the canopy of the oak, carrying something impractical in her beak, as she often did. A tuft of white fluff, probably thistle. He made a small sound to say hi. She made one back.

Below them, the campus was changing: the daytime giants drifting toward their dens, the nighttime ones emerging with their bright rectangle lights and their coffee cups and their slightly unhinged energy. Someone somewhere was playing music. Someone was running, though not from anything, just running, the way giants sometimes did, for reasons that had taken him months to accept were simply because of joy.

He looked at the nest. At the silk and the moss and the dandelion fluff they had pressed together in the early blue mornings. He looked at his mate beside him, the emerald-gold flash of her as she preened. He thought: he had never attended school, let alone know what it was. He had never sat in a fluorescent room or wept over a midterm or practiced the same speech until his voice broke and reformed. But he had watched. He had watched them love each other with reckless and huge hearts, watched them fail and restart, watched them sit on blankets and say nothing and mean everything. He had watched a girl evolve into her new self.

And now he understood. He thought that maybe this was what the stone buildings were for, not just for the knowledge that got spread inside them, but for all of this happening outside them. Besides the dead philosophers, the very alive human experience. The love that wouldn’t last but mattered anyway. The friendships that held. College was the place humans came to practice being alive before they had to do it for real.

He prayed to Demetera. He moved a little closer to his mate to snuggle.

The campus glittered below them like a small but loud, temporary yet irreplaceable world.
